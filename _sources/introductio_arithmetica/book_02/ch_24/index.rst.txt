=================================================================================
Chapter 24: The Geometric Proportion, Platonic Solid Bonding & Square Differences
=================================================================================

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0285
   :page: p. 269

**1.** Next after the arithmetic stands the continuous geometric mean, which alone is strictly called :term:`proportion <analogia>` because the terms within it are seen to stand to one another in the very same ratio.
It arises whenever, among three or more terms, as the greatest stands to the term next below it, so that term stands to the one beneath it—and if there be still more terms, that one likewise to the next beneath—so that they differ from one another not by the same quantity, but by the same quality of ratio, contrary to what we observed in the arithmetic proportion.

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0286
   :page: p. 270

For example, let us set out the numbers advancing from unity in double ratio, :math:`1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64` and so forth without end, or in triple ratio, :math:`1, 3, 9, 27, 81, 243` and onward, or in quadruple ratio, or in any series akin to those displayed.
In each of these rows, three consecutive terms, or four, or as many as one chooses to take, will yield the geometric proportion toward one another: as the first is to the one below it, so is that one to the one below it, and that in turn to the one still further below, as far as one wishes, and even promiscuously.

For instance, take :math:`2, 4, 8`: the ratio that :math:`8` has to :math:`4`, :math:`4` also has to :math:`2`, and conversely; yet they do not keep an equal quantity between them. Or again take :math:`2, 4, 8, 16`: not only does :math:`16` keep the same ratio to :math:`8` as before, even without equal difference,
but even promiscuously it preserves a like relation: as :math:`16` is to :math:`4`, so is :math:`8` to :math:`2`, and conversely as :math:`2` is to :math:`8`, so is :math:`4` to :math:`16`; disjunctively, as :math:`2` is to :math:`4`, so is :math:`8` to :math:`16`; and inverted and disjunctively, as :math:`16` is to :math:`8`, so is :math:`4` to :math:`2`, for it keeps the double ratio.

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0287
   :page: p. 271

**2.** The geometric mean has a property of its own belonging to none of the others: the differences between the terms stand to one another in the same ratio in which the consecutive terms themselves stand, the greater to the lesser, and conversely as the converse terms.
It has yet a further peculiarity: the greater terms have as their difference from the lesser terms those lesser terms themselves, and likewise difference differs from difference by the lesser difference itself when the terms are laid out in double ratio; in triple ratio the terms and differences will have a difference equal to twice the term below; in quadruple ratio thrice, in quintuple four times, and so on without end.

**3.** Geometric proportions arise not only among multiples, but in all forms of superparticulars, superpartients, and mixed ratios; and the preeminent property of this mean holds across all of them:
when the terms are continuous, the product of the extremes equals the square of the mean; when they stand in disjunct order, or among more terms—even if not continuous, so long as they are even in number—the product of the extremes equals the product of the means.

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0288
   :page: p. 272

**4.** As a sufficient and self-contained example that this property of geometric proportion holds across all relations—every kind of multiple, superparticular, superpartient, and mixed—let that construction suffice where from equality I generated by the three rules all the forms of inequality from one another, both when set in direct order and when reversed.
For every single construction and arrangement is a geometric proportion possessing all the properties just stated, together with a fourth: that it preserves the very same ratio both in the greater terms and in the lesser.

Furthermore, if we lay out the combined row of oblongs and squares alternating one by one so as to include the terms of both series, and then cut them off in threes starting from unity—always making the last term of the preceding triad the first term of the next—we shall discover emerging from the multiple relation (namely the double) all the superparticulars in succession: first the sesquialter, then the sesquitertian, then the sesquiquartan, and so onward.

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0289
   :page: p. 273

**5.** Here, moreover, is the most fitting occasion to recall a corollary that serves us toward a certain Platonic theorem. Plane numbers are always held together by one mean, whereas solid numbers are bound by two proportional means. Between two consecutive squares, one mean alone is found preserving the geometric proportion—antecedent to the lesser and consequent to the greater—and never more than one; thus we observe two intervals toward either extreme from the mean itself in a relation of like ratios.
Again, between two consecutive cubes, two proportional mean terms alone are found according to geometric proportion, and never more: thus there are three intervals—one between the two means toward one another, and two between the extremes and the means on either side. Hence solid figures are said to have three dimensions, and plane figures two. For example, :math:`1` and :math:`4` are plane numbers, with :math:`2` as their proportional mean; or :math:`4` and :math:`9` are two squares, and :math:`6` is their proportional mean, held by the greater in the same ratio in which it holds the lesser, and in which difference also stands to difference.
The reason for this is that the sides of the two squares—one proper side for each—both together generated :math:`6` itself. Among cubes, however, such as :math:`8` and :math:`27`, no longer one mean, but two are found, namely :math:`12` and :math:`18`, bringing both themselves and the extremes into the same ratio—the very ratio in which their differences also stand to one another; and the reason for this is that the two means are a mixed combination of the cubic sides: :math:`2 \times 2 \times 3` and :math:`3 \times 3 \times 2`.
In general, then, whenever a square multiplies a square, it invariably produces a square; if a square multiplies an oblong, it never produces a square; if a cube multiplies a cube, a cube will always result; and if an oblong multiplies a cube, never a cube—just as, of course, if an even number multiplies an even number, an even number is always generated, and if an odd multiplies an odd, always an odd, whereas if an odd multiplies an even or an even an odd, the product will always be even and never odd. Yet these matters will receive their proper elucidation when we read Plato together, in the passage on the so-called Nuptial Number in the *Republic* introduced in the person of the Muses. Let us now pass on to the third proportion, called the harmonic, and distinguish its nature.
