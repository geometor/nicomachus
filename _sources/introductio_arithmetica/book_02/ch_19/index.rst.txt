==========================================================================
Chapter 19: Alternating Weave & Harmonic Identities of Squares and Oblongs
==========================================================================

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0275
   :page: p. 259

**1.** That we may also be vividly persuaded of what has been stated—that existing things were framed out of warring and contrary elements and rightly received harmony (for harmony always arises from opposites, being a union of diverse elements and a concord of disagreeing minds)—let us set out in two parallel horizontal rows no longer the even numbers from 2 and the odd numbers from 1 by themselves, as just now, but the figures formed by summing those very numbers: squares from the odd numbers, and heteromecics from the even. As we study their display, we shall marvel at their mutual friendliness and joint cooperation in bringing forth and completing all other things, so that we may justly conclude that in the nature of the whole cosmos as well, something of this sort is brought to pass by cosmic providence.

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0276
   :page: p. 260

**2.** Let the two rows be these:

* Squares from unity: 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144, 169, 196, 225
* Heteromecics beginning from 2: 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, 42, 56, 72, 90, 110, 132, 156, 182, 210, 240

First, the first term of the latter row to the first of the former is the root multiple (double, 2 : 1), the second to the second is sesquialter (6 : 4), the third to the third is sesquitertian (12 : 9), the fourth to the fourth is sesquiquartan (20 : 16), then sesquiquintan (30 : 25), sesquisextan (42 : 36), and so to infinity in due proportion; meanwhile their differences advance along the unbroken number series from unity—1 for the first pair, 2 for the second, 3 for the third, and always so.

**3.** Next, if the second square (4) is paired with the first heteromecic (2), the third (9) with the second (6), the fourth (16) with the third (12), and the rest in order, they preserve the exact same ratios unchanged as before (4 : 2, 9 : 6, 16 : 12), yet those same differences no longer start from 1, but advance starting from 2. Moreover, as we move forward in the first pairing, the first stands to the first as the first root multiple (2 : 1), the second to the second as the second from the root sesquialter (6 : 4), the third to the third as the third from the root sesquitertian (12 : 9), and the succeeding pairs progress in the same manner.

**4.** Again, the squares have only the odd numbers as differences among themselves, while the heteromecics have the even numbers. And if we insert the first heteromecic (2) as mean between the first two squares (1 and 4), the second (6) between the next pair (4 and 9), the third (12) between the following pair (9 and 16), and the fourth (20) between the next (16 and 25), still more orderly relationships appear in each triad.

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0277
   :page: p. 261

For whatever ratio 4 has to 2, that same ratio 2 has to 1; as 9 is sesquialter to 6, so 6 is to 4; as 16 is to 12, so 12 is to 9; and thus onward as numbers and ratios progress regularly. For as the greatest term is to the middle, so will the middle be to the least—not in a single fixed ratio, but in a varied ratio advancing systematically. In every such triad, the product of the extremes equals the square of the mean; and adding the extremes once together with twice the mean will invariably yield a square. And most charming of all, by adding them together alternately—1 and 2, 2 and 4, 4 and 6, 6 and 9, 9 and 12, 12 and 16, 16 and 20, and always so—there arises in order the generation of the triangular numbers (3, 6, 10, 15, 21, 28, 36), signifying that the nature of these two is even more primal than the principle of all polygons.
