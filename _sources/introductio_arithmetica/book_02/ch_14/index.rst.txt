============================================================
Chapter 14: Truncated, Bi-Truncated & Tri-Truncated Pyramids
============================================================

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0266
   :page: p. 250

**1.** Next come the pyramids that rise in uniform figure from a square base toward one and the same summit. These are shaped in the exact same manner as the triangular pyramids already described. Setting out the regular squares from unity in a row—1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, and those following—I stack them in heaps one atop another in depth: placing 1 on top of 4, I obtain 5, the first pyramid in actuality from a square base, since here too unity is first in potentiality. Again, I set this very pyramid of five units, just as it stands, entire upon the square 9, and there is formed for me the pyramid 14 on a square base, having 3 as its side on every edge, whereas the preceding one, 5, had side 2, and the primal one in potentiality had side 1;

   :page: p. 251

for here as well every side of any pyramid must measure as many units as the count of polygons stacked together to build it. Once more, I set the whole pyramid 14, whose base is the square 9, upon the square 16, and 30 is completed—the third pyramid in actuality among those on a square base. By the very same ordering and procedure, from a pentagonal base, a hexagonal base, a heptagonal base, and advancing ever further, we shall construct pyramids by stacking upon each other the polygons proper to each class, beginning from unity as the least and proceeding indefinitely in each kind.

**2.** From this too it grows plain that triangles are more elementary; for all pyramids without exception, whatever their several polygonal bases, are enclosed by triangles right up to the summit.

**3.** So that we may not be unfamiliar with *truncated*, *bi-truncated*, and *tri-truncated* pyramids—terms we shall meet especially in theoretical writings—you must know that if a pyramid rising by accumulation from any base whatever (that is, having any polygon as its base, whether triangle, square, pentagon, or any subsequent polygon of the same kind) fails to taper to unity, it is called simply *truncated*, being robbed of the natural crowning summit common to all;

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0268
   :page: p. 252

for it does not terminate in the polygon in potentiality, unity, as in a single point, but in another polygon in actuality, so that its top is no longer a unit, but a plane surface equiangular with its base serves as its boundary. And if, besides not ending in unity, it also fails to end in the first actual polygon after unity, such a solid is called *bi-truncated*; if furthermore it lacks the second actual polygon at its finish, stopping at the one below it, it shall be called *tri-truncated*, and *tetra-truncated* if it lacks the next as well, and *penta-truncated* in turn, extending the name as far as you please.
