=====================================
Chapter 8: The Even-Times Even Number
=====================================

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0208
   :page: p. 192

**1.** Every number is half of the sum of the two lying adjacent to it on either side, and likewise half of the sum of those standing one beyond on either side, and again of those beyond them, and so on as far as it is possible to go.

**2.** Alone among all, the :term:`monad <monas>`—because it does not have two numbers flanking it on either side—is half of the single number lying beside it; therefore the monad is by nature the starting-point of all.

**3.** Under subdivision of the **even**, one species is the **even-times even**, another the **odd-times even**, and a third the **even-times odd**. The even-times even and the even-times odd stand opposed to one another like extremes, while the odd-times even is common to both, like a mean between them.

**4.** Now an **even-times even** number is one that is itself capable of being split into two equal parts in accordance with the nature of its genus, and has either of its halves likewise divisible into two, and again in the same manner either of those parts divisible into two equal halves, until the division of successive subdivisions arrives at the

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0209
   :page: p. 193

naturally indivisible monad.

**5.** Let :math:`64` serve as an example: its half is :math:`32`, of this the half is :math:`16`, of this the half is :math:`8`, of this :math:`4`, and of this :math:`2`; then last of all the monad is half of this, and being by nature indivisible it no longer admits a half.

**6.** It follows as a property of this number that whatever part is taken in it has an even-times even name in its designation, and that same part is also even-times even in the multitude of its monads, neither of the two ever sharing with the other genus.

**7.** And perhaps it is for this very reason that it has been named *even-times even*: because, being itself even, it always has its parts, and the parts of its parts down to the monad, even both in name and in value—or in other words, every part it possesses is even-times even in name, and the same part is also even-times even in value.

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0210
   :page: p. 194

**8–9.** The generation of the even-times even—so that none may escape, but all fall under a single rule—takes place thus: as one advances from the monad as from a root in the double ratio without end, all the numbers that arise are even-times even, and it is impossible to find any others besides these; for example:

.. math::

   1,\ 2,\ 4,\ 8,\ 16,\ 32,\ 64,\ 128,\ 256,\ 512,\ \dots

and so on as far as one wishes.

**10.** Each of the terms set forth has come into being through the continuous double ratio from the monad, and each is altogether even-times even; moreover, every part that it is found to have is always named after

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0211
   :page: p. 195

one of the terms before it, and the assembly of monads contained in that part is equal to one of the terms before it, yet by reciprocal inversion and exchange. If the terms set forth by doubling from the monad are even in count, no single middle term can be found, but invariably two, from which the reciprocal exchange of parts with values and values with parts proceeds in order—first to the two adjacent on either side, then to those lying beyond on either side, until it reaches the outermost extremes, so that the whole is named after the monad and the monad after the whole. For instance, if we take :math:`128` as the greatest term, the terms set out will be even in count—eight in all up to it—and they will not have one middle term (which is impossible in an even count), but necessarily two, :math:`8` and :math:`16`, which answer to one another alternately. For of the whole :math:`128`, the eighth part is :math:`16`, and conversely the sixteenth part is :math:`8`. Proceeding outward on either side, the fourth part is :math:`32` and the thirty-second part is :math:`4`; again, the half is :math:`64` and the sixty-fourth part is :math:`2`; and finally at the extremes, the hundred-and-twenty-eighth part is :math:`1`, while conversely the whole, corresponding to the monad, is :math:`128`.

**11.** If the array is set forth in an odd count of terms—say seven, when we choose :math:`64`—there will necessarily be a single middle term in accordance with the nature of odd numbers, and this term answers to itself because it has no partner, while those flanking it on either side always answer to one another until the correspondence ends at the extremes: thus the sixty-fourth part will be :math:`1` and the whole corresponding to :math:`1` will be :math:`64`; the half will be :math:`32` and the thirty-second part :math:`2`; the fourth part :math:`16` and the sixteenth part :math:`4`; and the eighth part, without reciprocal exchange, is :math:`8` itself.

**12.** It is a property of all these terms when added together in a heap that they equal the term immediately after them minus a monad, so that in every case the sum will necessarily be an odd number; for whatever falls short of an even number by a monad is always odd.

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0212
   :page: p. 196

**13.** This observation will shortly be of service to us for the construction of :term:`perfect numbers <teleios>`. For example, all the terms below :math:`256` down to the monad, when summed together, equal :math:`256` minus one monad; all those below :math:`128`, the term right beneath it, likewise equal :math:`128` minus one monad; and in the same way for every successive term, just as the monad itself equals the term after it (namely :math:`2`) minus a monad, and both together equal the next term minus a monad, and all three together equal the one after that minus a monad; and advancing to infinity you will find this unfailing.

**14.** This too is most necessary to remember: if the terms set out of the chosen even-times even number are even in count, the product of the extremes multiplied together will always equal the product of the means multiplied together; and if the count of terms is odd, the product of the extremes equals the square of the middle term multiplied by itself. Thus in an even count of terms, :math:`1 \times 128` equals :math:`8 \times 16`, and also :math:`2 \times 64`, and again :math:`4 \times 32`, throughout the entire series; while in an odd count, :math:`1 \times 64` equals :math:`2 \times 32`, and this equals :math:`4 \times 16`, and this again equals :math:`8 \times 8`, the single mean multiplied by itself.
