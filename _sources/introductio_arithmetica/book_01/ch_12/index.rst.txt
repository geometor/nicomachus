===========================================
Chapter 12: Secondary and Composite Numbers
===========================================

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0218
   :page: p. 202

**1.** A secondary and composite number is indeed odd, since it is distinguished out of one and the same genus, yet it possesses nothing primary or

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0219
   :page: p. 203

elemental in itself; for it has received its origin when another number is combined. Therefore, alongside the part bearing its own name, it comes to possess one or more parts with different names: the part sharing its own name is, as in every case, unity, whereas the differently named part or parts are never unity, but always that very number or those numbers by whose combination it was formed—for example, 9, 15, 21, 25, 27, 33, 35, 39.
Each of these is measured by unity just as the others are, and has a part named after itself just as they do by reason of the nature of their shared genus; yet in a distinct and more specific way it also employs a part or parts with different names: 9 has a third besides its ninth, 15 a third and a fifth besides its fifteenth, 21 a seventh and a third besides its twenty-first, and 25 employs the differently named fifth alongside its own twenty-fifth.

**2.** Now it is termed secondary because it can also employ another measure together with unity, and because it is not primal, but arose when another number was added to itself or combined with another—9 from 3, 15 from 5 or indeed from 3, and the succeeding ones in the same manner. And it is called composite for this reason: that it can be resolved back into the very numbers out of which it was constituted, inasmuch as it can also be measured by them; and nothing capable of resolution is incomposite, but altogether composite.
