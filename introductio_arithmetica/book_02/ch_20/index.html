Chapter 20: Generation of Cubes from Consecutive Odd Blocks¶

1. Furthermore, every square becomes a heteromecic when its own side is added to it—or, by Zeus, when its own side is subtracted from it. Thus the Other is conceived both in excess and in deficiency relative to the Same, seeing that it is produced by addition and subtraction, just as the two species of the unequal—the greater and the lesser—derive their generation from addition or subtraction applied to the equal.

2. This too provides sufficient proof that both kinds share in sameness and otherness—in otherness indefinitely, and in sameness determinately: generically the monad and the dyad, and subordinately the odd sharing in sameness because it is akin to the monad, and the even in otherness because of the dyad.

f. 0278 · p. 262

3. Still more clearly, the square is related to sameness because it is a sum of odd numbers, and the heteromecic to otherness because of the even; for like friends in those two rows, these two kinds exchange with each other by turns either the same differences—though not the same ratios—or conversely the same ratios, though not the same differences. For what stands in double ratio between 4 and 2 stands in superparticular ratio between 6 and 4; and again what stands in sesquialter ratio between 9 and 6 stands in sesquitertian ratio between 12 and 9, and always so: whatever is the same in quality is other in quantity, and conversely whatever is the same in quantity is other in quality.

4. Moreover, across every one of these relations, the identical difference between the two terms will necessarily be named as a part differing by one: being half of one term and a third of the other, or a third of one and a fourth of the other, or a fourth of one and a fifth of the other, and so in sequence.

5. Yet what will confirm most decisively that the odd—and never the even—is the primary cause of sameness must be shown in every proportional series from unity. For instance, in the double series 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, and in the triple series 1, 3, 9, 27, 81, 243, 729, 2187, and as far as you please, you will find of necessity that all terms in odd positions are squares, and none others by any device, while not a single term in an even position is a square.

f. 0279 · p. 263

Indeed, all the equally-times-equal-times-equal numbers—that is, cubes—even though they are three-dimensional and appear to partake more fully of solidity, are the offspring of odd numbers, never of even: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, and those advancing proportionally—and that by a simple, unvaried rule! Set out the consecutive odd numbers from unity to infinity and observe: the first makes the cube in potentiality (1); the next two added together make the second cube (3 + 5 = 8); the three after those make the third (7 + 9 + 11 = 27); the four following these make the fourth (13 + 15 + 17 + 19 = 64); the five after these make the fifth (21 + 23 + 25 + 27 + 29 = 125); the next six make the sixth (31 + 33 + 35 + 37 + 39 + 41 = 216); and this continues forever.
