Chapter 25: The Harmonic Proportion (Subcontrary) & Extreme-Product Law¶

1. The mean ranked third in order is called the harmonic. It occurs among three terms when the middle term stands toward the extremes neither in the same ratio—being consequent to the one and antecedent to the other, as in the geometric—nor with equal intervals and differing ratios, as in the arithmetic. Rather, it occurs when, as the greatest term stands to the least, so stands the difference between the greatest and the middle term to the difference between the middle term and the least—for example, \(3, 4, 6\), or again \(2, 3, 6\).

f. 0290 · p. 274

For \(6\) exceeds \(4\) by a third of itself—since \(2\) is one-third of \(6\)—and \(3\) falls short of \(4\) by a third of itself, for unity is one-third of \(3\). In the former example the extremes lie in double ratio, and their differences from the mean stand again to each other in the same double ratio; in the second example each stands in triple ratio. 2. It possesses a property subcontrary, as I said before, to that of the arithmetic proportion. There, the ratios among the lesser terms were greater and those among the greater terms were smaller; here, on the contrary, the ratios among the greater terms are greater and those among the lesser terms are smaller, so that in the geometric—standing as the midpoint between both—equality of the ratios on either side is fittingly beheld as the boundary between the greater and the less.

f. 0291 · p. 275

3. Furthermore, in the arithmetic proportion the middle term appears greater and less than those on either side by the same fraction of itself, but by one fraction and another of those extremes themselves; whereas in this harmonic proportion it is the contrary: the middle term is greater and less than the terms on either side by different fractions of itself, yet always by the very same fraction of those outer terms themselves—for example, by half of each, or by a third of both. And the geometric, as standing on the frontier between both, displays this equality neither in the mean alone nor in the extremes alone, but in both mean and extreme.

f. 0292 · p. 276

4. Moreover, the harmonic has this peculiar accompaniment: the extremes added together and multiplied by the mean produce twice what comes from multiplying them by one another.

f. 0293 · p. 277

5. This mean was called harmonic because, while the arithmetic was distinguished by quantity—providing equality in that respect in the interval of its terms toward one another—and the geometric by quality—yielding like relations according to the quality of the terms toward one another—this one appears in relation to something else in diverse forms, residing neither in the terms alone nor in the differences alone, but partly in the terms and partly in the differences: as the greatest is to the least, so is the difference of the greatest toward the mean next to it to the difference of the least toward that same mean, and conversely.
