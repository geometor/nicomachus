Chapter 29: The Most Perfect Proportion (6 : 8 :: 9 : 12)¶

1. It remains for me to explain briefly the most perfect proportion, three-dimensional and embracing all the others, since it is most useful for every advance in music and natural philosophy. For this alone among the rest could properly and truly be called harmony, since it is not planar nor bound together by a single mean alone, but by two, so as to extend in three dimensions—just as the cube was lately explained to be harmony.

2. Whenever, then, between two extreme terms—both three-dimensional, whether formed equally by equal by equal so as to be cubes, or equally by equal by unequal so as to be beams or plinths, or unequally by unequal by unequal so as to be scalene—two other middle terms are found preserving alternately and promiscuously the same ratios toward the extremes, so that while one maintains the harmonic proportion, the other completes the arithmetic: it follows of necessity, when the four terms are so disposed, that the geometric proportion also shines forth interwoven and tested alongside both means: as the greatest is to the third from it, so is the second below it to the fourth—for such an arrangement makes the product of the means equal to the product of the extremes.

f. 0301 · p. 285

Again, if the greatest term stands to the one below it in the same difference as this latter stands to the least, such an examination reveals the arithmetic proportion, and the sum of the extremes is double that mean. And if the third term from the greatest exceeds and is exceeded by the same fractional part of the extremes themselves, that is harmonic, and the product of the mean with the sum of the extremes is twice the product of the extremes.

3. Let our example of it be \(6, 8, 9, 12\). Here \(6\) is a scalene solid from \(1 \times 2 \times 3\), and \(12\) from \(2 \times 2 \times 3\) extended in three dimensions; while of the middle terms the lesser comes from \(1 \times 2 \times 4\) and the greater from \(1 \times 3 \times 3\). Thus the extremes are three-dimensional solids and the means are of like kind with them. By the geometric, as \(12\) is to \(8\), so is \(9\) to \(6\); by the arithmetic, by how much \(12\) exceeds \(9\), by so much does \(9\) exceed \(6\); and by the harmonic, by what fractional part of \(6\) itself \(8\) exceeds \(6\), by that same fractional part of \(12\) itself is it exceeded by \(12\).

Moreover, \(8 : 6\) or \(12 : 9\) gives the fourth in sesquitertian ratio; \(9 : 6\) or \(12 : 8\) gives the fifth in sesquialter ratio; and \(12 : 6\) gives the octave in double ratio. And what remains, \(9 : 8\), is the interval of a whole tone (toniaion) in superoctave ratio—which serves as the common measure of all ratios in music, being indeed better known because it is in fact the difference between the first and most elementary concords toward one another.

f. 0302 · p. 286

4. Concerning the phenomena and properties inherent in numbers, let thus much suffice as a first introduction.
