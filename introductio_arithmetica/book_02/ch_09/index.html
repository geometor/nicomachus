Chapter 9: Square Numbers and Odd-Number Gnomons¶

1. Next after this is the square number, displaying in its diagram not three angles like the previous one, but four, yet still in equilateral arrangement—such as \(1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100\). Their equilateral square diagrams take shape thus:

α    α α    α α α    α α α α    α α α α α
             α α    α α α    α α α α    α α α α α
                    α α α    α α α α    α α α α α
                             α α α α    α α α α α
                                        α α α α α

and so onward as far as you desire.

2. With these also it holds, just as with the preceding ones, that the advance of their sides proceeds along the natural numbers: the side

f. 0259 · p. 243

of the first potential square, \(1\), is \(1\); of the first actual square, \(4\), the side is \(2\); of the second actual, \(9\), the side is \(3\); of the third actual after it, \(16\), the side is \(4\); of the fourth, \(5\); of the fifth, \(6\); and universally so onward in order. 3. This number too is generated when the natural number series is set out in a row and there are accumulated upon unity not the consecutive terms—as was shown before—but all those lying one apart, namely the odd numbers. Thus \(1\) is the first potential square; \(1 + 3\) is the first actual square; \(1 + 3 + 5\) is the second actual square; \(1 + 3 + 5 + 7\) is the third actual square; the next arises when \(9\) is piled onto the former ones, the one after it when \(11\) is added, and so always. And in these also, the side of each contains as many units as the numbers accumulated for its generation.
