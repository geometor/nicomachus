Chapter 27: Insertion of the Three Classical Means Between Two Extremes¶

1. Just as on the divided musical canon—when a single stretched string or a flute of fixed length is laid out with immovable ends, while the intermediate position is shifted on the flute by finger-holes or on the string by a movable bridge—each of the three means just described, arithmetic, geometric, and harmonic, can be produced one after another, so that they are fittingly and most truly named from this shifting and transfer of the middle term: so too between any two numerical terms, whether both be odd or both be even, it is both reasonable and possible, while those extremes remain fixed and unshifted, to insert a mean conforming to each of the three proportions—exceeding and exceeded by an equal amount in the arithmetic, set apart by a like ratio in the geometric, and greater and less by the very same fractional part of the extremes themselves in the harmonic.

2. Let two even terms be set out first, between which we must seek how the three means may be ranged and what numbers they are: let them be \(10\) and \(40\). First, then, I insert the arithmetic mean, which is \(25\), and all its properties are preserved here too: as each term is to itself, so is difference to difference, namely in equality; by as much as the greater exceeds the mean, by so much does the mean exceed the less; the sum of the extremes is double the mean; the ratio of the lesser terms is greater than that of the greater; the product of the extremes is less than the square of the mean by the square of the differences; and the mean is greater and less than the extremes by the same fraction of itself, though by different fractions when measured in the extremes.

f. 0296 · p. 280

3. Now if I insert \(20\) as the mean between these same even terms, the properties of the geometric proportion emerge, while those of the arithmetic vanish: as the greater is to the mean, so is the mean to the small term; the product of the extremes equals the square of the mean; the differences stand to one another in the same ratio as the terms; the identity of fraction in excess and defect lies neither in the pure extremes nor in the pure mean, but alternately in the mean and one of the extremes; and the ratio is equal both among greater terms and among lesser. 4. If, on the other hand, I substitute \(16\) as the middle term, the properties of the two former means withdraw, and those of the harmonic come to light while the same two even extremes remain fixed: as the greatest is to the least, so is the difference of the greater terms to that of the lesser; by whatever fractional parts of the greatest itself the mean is less than the greatest, by so many fractional parts of the least itself is that same mean greater than the least; the ratio among the greater terms is greater and that among the lesser is smaller, which happens in no other mean; and the extremes summed and multiplied by the mean yield twice the product of the extremes. 5. And if the two extreme terms set down are not even, but odd—for example, \(5\) and \(45\)—the same term \(25\) will form the arithmetic mean, because the extremes rise above and fall below it by an equal number, preserving the same quantity of difference toward it. Substituting \(15\) yields the geometric, since it is both triple the one and one-third of the other. And \(9\), taking the middle place, yields the harmonic: for by the very fractional parts by which it exceeds the lesser—four-fifths of that lesser term itself—by those same fractional parts is it less than the greater when measured in the greater itself, namely four-fifths; and if you test all the properties stated earlier, you will find them in agreement.

f. 0297 · p. 281

6. Let this be your rule for skillfully constructing the terms just shown according to the three proportions. For both odd and even terms placed before you, you will find the arithmetic mean by adding the extremes and setting half their sum in the middle, or by bisecting the excess of the greater over the less and adding that to the less. For the geometric, find the square root of the oblong product of the extremes and make it the middle term; or else observe the ratio the extremes bear to one another, bisect that ratio, and form the mean—for instance, the double in the case of a quadruple ratio. For the harmonic, multiply the difference of the extremes by the lesser term, divide the product by the sum of the extremes, and add the quotient to the lesser term; the result will be the harmonic mean.
