Chapter 21: Nature of Proportion, Ratio, Continuous & Disjunct Forms — Summary¶

Overview¶

Nicomachus opens the culminating final movement of the Introductio Arithmetica by defining proportion (analogia) and classifying its structural forms across three or more terms:

  1. Strict vs. Broad Proportion (II.21.1–3): A ratio (logos) is the relation (schesis) of two terms to one another; a proportion (analogia) is strictly the combination (syllepsis) of two or more ratios (e.g., \(1 : 2 :: 2 : 4\)), and broadly the combination of two or more relations governed by a common difference rather than a common ratio. Hence three terms form the minimum proportion, extendable indefinitely (\(1, 2, 4, 8, 16, \dots\)).

  2. Continuous Proportion (*Synēmmenē*, II.21.5): A single middle term serves simultaneously as consequent to the greater extreme and antecedent to the lesser—either in quality of ratio (\(1, 2, 4\), where \(4:2 = 2:1\)) or in quantity of difference (\(1, 2, 3\), where \(3 - 2 = 2 - 1\)).

  3. Disjunct Proportion (*Diezeugmenē*, II.21.6): Four or more terms employ two distinct middle terms—one antecedent to the lesser, the other consequent to the greater—preserving both direct, inverse, and alternating relations in quality (\(1, 2, 4, 8\): \(2:1 = 8:4\) and \(1:4 = 2:8\)) and in quantity (\(1, 2, 3, 4\): \(2 - 1 = 4 - 3\) and \(3 - 1 = 4 - 2\)).
