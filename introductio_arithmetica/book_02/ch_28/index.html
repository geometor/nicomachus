Chapter 28: The Seven Additional Means Completing the Ten Proportions¶

1. So much, then, concerning the three proportions celebrated among the ancients, which I have deliberately set forth more clearly and more fully because we meet them frequently and in varied guises throughout our readings. Those that follow must be treated more concisely, since they are not commonly found in the ancient writers, yet are taken up for our own practice and to round out, as it were, the completeness of our reasoning. 2. I shall present these in order according to their contrariety to the three archetypal means already expounded, since they are fashioned out of those very three and obtain a corresponding sequence.

3. The fourth, also named the subcontrary because it is opposed to and acts inversely to the harmonic, arises when, among three terms, as the greatest stands to the least, so stands the difference of the lesser terms to the difference of the greater—for example, \(3, 5, 6\), where the ratios compared are seen to be double. The manner in which it is opposed to the harmonic is plain: with the same extremes in both, and in double ratio too, in the previous proportion the excess of the greater terms kept that same ratio to the difference of the lesser, whereas here, conversely, the difference of the lesser keeps it to that of the greater.

f. 0298 · p. 282

One must also note this property of it: the product of the greatest and middle terms is double the product of the middle and least, for \(6 \times 5\) is double \(5 \times 3\).

4. Two means, the fifth and the sixth, were both fashioned after the geometric, yet differ from one another thus. The fifth occurs when, among three terms, as the middle term stands to the least, so stands their difference to the difference between the greatest and the middle term—for example, \(2, 4, 5\); for \(4\) is double \(2\) (middle term to least), and \(2\) is double \(1\) (difference of lesser terms to difference of greater). What renders it subcontrary to the geometric is this: in the geometric, as the mean is to the lesser, so is the excess of the greater over the mean to the excess of the mean over the lesser, whereas here, conversely, it is the difference of the lesser to that of the greater. Still, this too has a property of its own: the product of the greatest and middle terms is double the product of the greatest and least, for \(5 \times 4\) is double \(5 \times 2\).

5. The sixth occurs when, among three terms, as the greatest stands to the middle term, so stands the excess of the middle over the least to the excess of the greatest over the middle—for example, \(1, 4, 6\), where each pair is in sesquialter ratio. Here too the cause of its contrariety to the geometric is similar, for the likeness of ratios is inverted here just as in the fifth.

f. 0299 · p. 283

6. These, then, are the six means celebrated among earlier writers: three archetypal ones enduring from Pythagoras down to Plato and Aristotle, and three others subcontrary to them that came into use among the commentators and followers after them.

7. Certain thinkers, however, by shifting the terms and differences of these, devised four additional means that scarcely appear in the writings of the ancients, as being somewhat overly subtle refinements; yet so that I may not seem ignorant of them, I must run through them briefly as follows. 8. The first of these—seventh in the complete arrangement—occurs when, as the greatest stands to the least, so stands their difference to the difference of the lesser terms, as \(6, 8, 9\); for the sesquialter ratio appears in both comparisons.

9. The eighth mean, which is the second of this group, arises when, as the greatest stands to the least, so stands the difference of the extremes to the difference of the greater terms, as \(6, 7, 9\); for this too keeps both ratios sesquialter.

f. 0300 · p. 284

10. The ninth in the complete arrangement—third in the count of these newly devised means—arises when, among three terms, whatever ratio the middle term bears to the least, that same ratio the excess of the extremes bears to the excess of the least terms, as \(4, 6, 7\).

11. The tenth of all—fourth in the list of the later writers—is observed when, among three terms, as the middle stands to the least, so stands the difference of the extremes to the difference of the greater, as \(3, 5, 8\); for in each pair the ratio is superpartient (\(5 : 3\)).

12. In summary, let the terms of all ten proportions be set out in a single table for ready inspection: first \(1, 2, 3\); second \(1, 2, 4\); third \(3, 4, 6\); fourth \(3, 5, 6\); fifth \(2, 4, 5\); sixth \(1, 4, 6\); seventh \(6, 8, 9\); eighth \(6, 7, 9\); ninth \(4, 6, 7\); tenth \(3, 5, 8\).
