Chapter 1: Philosophy as Love of Wisdom & Immaterial Reality — Notes¶

Archival Witnesses & Apparatus¶

  • English Witness: Martin Luther D’Ooge (trans.), Nicomachus of Gerasa: Introduction to Arithmetic (University of Michigan Studies, Vol. XVI, 1926), pp. 181–182 (folios introductio_arithmetica:0197–0198).

  • Greek Witness: Richard Hoche (ed.), Nicomachi Geraseni Pythagorei Introductionis Arithmeticae Libri II (Leipzig: Teubner, 1866), pp. 1–3 (folios hoche_1866:0019–0021).

  • Boethius Parallel: Boethius, De Institutione Arithmetica I.1 (boethius:friedlein:1867, pp. 7–8 Friedlein).

Philosophical Notes¶

  • Pythagorean Etymology of Philosophy: Nicomachus transmits the classical tradition (also attested in Cicero Tusc. V.3.8 and Diogenes Laertius I.12) that Pythagoras narrowed sophia from technical craftsmanship (techne) to the episteme of eternal immaterial reality.

  • Matter and Incorporeal Accidents (I.1.3): In In Nicomachi Arithmeticam, John Philoponus notes Ammonius’s critique of calling matter trepte kai alloiote (“changeable and alterable”) rather than treptike kai alloiotike (“capable of receiving change”), since Nicomachus blends Middle-Platonic dualism with Aristotelian categories (quality, quantity, relation, place, time).

  • Quadrivium Context: See Chapter 3 for the formal bifurcation of quantity into the four sister sciences (mathemata).
