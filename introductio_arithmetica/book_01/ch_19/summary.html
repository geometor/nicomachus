Chapter 19: Second Species of Inequality: The Superparticular — Summary¶

Overview¶

Nicomachus develops the superparticular (epimorios, \((n+1):n\)) and demonstrates its natural derivation from the multiples via a \(10 \times 10\) multiplication table:

  1. Definitions & First Two Subspecies (I.19.1–7): A superparticular contains the whole of the lesser number plus one aliquot part of it. When that part is a half, the ratio is sesquialter (\(3:2, 6:4, 9:6, 12:8, \dots\)); when a third, sesquitertian (\(4:3, 8:6, 12:9, \dots\)). The root terms (pythmenes) are consecutive integers, while the \(k\)-th pair has \(k - 1\) integers between them.

  2. The 10 × 10 Table & Priority of Multiples (I.19.8–16): Comparing rows/columns arranged in gnomonic gamma-shapes (\(\Gamma\)) and chi-crosses (\(\mathrm{X}\)), rows \(2, 3, 4, \dots\) compared with row \(1\) give multiples, whereas adjacent rows (\(3\) vs. \(2\), \(4\) vs. \(3\), etc.) give superparticulars.

  3. Square–Heteromecic Diagonal Identity (I.19.17–19): The main diagonal holds the squares \(n^2\) (\(1, 4, 9, \dots, 100\)), flanked by heteromecic (oblong) products \(n(n+1)\), exhibiting the Pythagorean identity \(n^2 + (n+1)^2 + 2n(n+1) = (2n+1)^2\) and \((n-1)n + n(n+1) + 2n^2 = (2n)^2\).
