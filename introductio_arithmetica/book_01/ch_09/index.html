Chapter 9: The Even-Times Odd Number¶

1. The even-times odd is a number that is itself even in genus, yet is specifically set over against the even-times even just described: it admits division into two equal

f. 0213 · p. 197

parts in accordance with their common genus, yet immediately has each of its halves indivisible into two equal parts—such as \(6, 10, 14, 18, 22, 26\), and the like; for immediately after each of these is bisected, its parts are found to be incapable of further bisection.

2. It happens to this number that every part found within it is opposite in name to its value, and every quantity of a part is opposite in value to its name; nor can the value of a part ever in any way be of the same kind as its own name. For example, in the single number \(18\), the half—which is named in an even fashion—is \(9\), which is odd in value; while conversely the third—formed with an odd name—is \(6\), which is even in value; by reversal, the sixth part is \(3\) and the ninth part is \(2\); and the same pattern will be found in all the rest.

3–4. And perhaps it is for this very reason that it received such a title: because, while being even, it immediately yields halves that are odd. Moreover, this number is generated when the numbers from the monad differing by two—that is, the odd numbers—are set forth in regular order and multiplied by two as far as one wishes. The numbers thus produced will arise in sequence as:

\[6,\ 10,\ 14,\ 18,\ 22,\ 26,\ 30,\ \dots\]

and so onward as far as you care to advance.

5. In this sequence the greater terms always differ from the lesser terms next to them by four. The cause of this is that their original generators—namely the odd numbers, which exceed one another by two—were multiplied by two to produce these, and two multiplying two makes four. Thus in the natural web of number, the even-times odd numbers will be found standing fifth from one another, exceeding by four, skipping three intervening terms, and generated by doubling the odd

f. 0214 · p. 198

numbers.

6. They are said to have properties opposite to those of the even-times even because of the latter only the greatest extreme is divisible, whereas of the former only the least term was indivisible; and furthermore because in the even-times even the reciprocal pairing of parts from the extremes toward a single mean or two means made the product of the extremes equal to the square of the mean or to the product of the two means; whereas in the even-times odd, under the same exchange and inspection, the single middle term is half the sum of the two extremes added together, or if there be two middle terms, both together equal the sum of the two extremes.
