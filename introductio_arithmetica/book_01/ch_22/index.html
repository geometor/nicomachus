Chapter 22: The Multiple Superparticular Species¶

1. The multiple superparticular is a relation in which the greater of the terms compared holds the lesser within itself more than once, and in addition some single part of it, whatever that part may be. As a compound, this species varies in two respects according to the proper character of each of the combined names. Because the multiple superparticular is compounded generally out of the multiple and the superparticular, in its specific subdivisions it will display variety and alternation—separately in the first part of the name, and specifically in the second: in the first, from the multiple, double, triple, quadruple, quintuple, and so on; in the second, from the superparticular generally, its orderly specific forms—sesquialter, sesquitertian, sesquiquartan, sesquiquintan, and so on—so that their compounding advances in such an order as this: double sesquialter, double sesquitertian, double sesquiquartan, double sesquiquintan, double sesquisextan, and analogously; starting anew, triple sesquialter, triple sesquitertian, triple sesquiquartan, triple sesquiquintan;

starting again from the top, quadruple sesquialter, quadruple sesquitertian, quadruple sesquiquartan, quadruple sesquiquintan; and again from the top, quintuple sesquialter, quintuple sesquitertian, quintuple sesquiquartan, quintuple sesquiquintan, and those corresponding to them to infinity.

2. For as many times as the greater contains the lesser in full, from that quantity will be named the first part of the compound name in the multiple superparticular; and whatever the fractional part may be that is present in the greater alongside the repeated whole, after that part will be named the second species of ratio out of which the multiple superparticular is composed. Examples of this are: 5 to 2, double sesquialter; 7 to 3, double sesquitertian; 9 to 4, double sesquiquartan; 11 to 5, double sesquiquintan.

3. You will always produce them in exact order if you compare the pure odd numbers from 5 onward against the successive even and odd numbers from 2 onward, first to first, second to second, third to third, and the remaining ones of equal rank to those of equal rank. All the successive numbers from 5 differing by 5, compared against the successive even numbers from 2, will be pure double sesquialters, matching rank by rank. Again, when all those from 3 differing by 3 are set out—3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21— and in another row those from 7 differing by 7 endlessly—7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42, 49—and the greater are compared to the lesser, first to first, second to second, third to third, fourth to fourth, and so onward, the second species, that of the double sesquitertians, appears set out with its proper regularity. Again, if from a fresh start the pure row of quadruples is set out—4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32—and alongside it in another row the continuous series starting from 9 and advancing by steps of 9—9, 18, 27, 36, 45, 54— we shall have once more appearing the specific multiple superparticular, namely the orderly double sesquiquartan; and anyone who wishes can carry this thought on to infinity.

4. The next species starts from the triple sesquialter, as 7 to 2, 14 to 4, and simply those advancing by sevens compared to the orderly even numbers from 2. Again from a fresh beginning, 10 to 3 is the first triple sesquitertian, 20 to 6 the second triple sesquitertian, and generally the successive tenfold multiples compared to the successive triples. This very order we can discern more accurately and clearly in the

table already studied: compared against the first row, all the succeeding rows arranged in order, whole compared to whole, exhibit the orderly species of the multiple endlessly when all are set against the same first row; compared against all those above them in succession, each against its neighbor, beginning from the second row, all the species of the superparticular are generated in their proper regularity; and from the third row, all the odd-numbered rows starting from the fifth, tested against the third and its successors term by term, display all the orderly species of the superpartient; while the comparisons of the multiple superparticular will hold a natural and proper order if, starting from the second row, we compare the numbers from the fifth row onward, first to first, second to second, third to third, and so on—against the third row those from the seventh, against the fourth those from the ninth, in fitting regularity as far as one has strength to follow. Clearly the lesser terms are here too reciprocally named with the prefix sub- toward the greater according to the designations laid down for all.
