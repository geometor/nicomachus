About This Edition¶

geometor.nicomachus establishes a definitive Digital Scriptorium edition of Nicomachus of Gerasa’s (c. 60–120 CE) Introductio Arithmetica (Ἀριθμητικὴ Εἰσαγωγή). Standing at the historical watershed between the rigorous axiomatic deductions of Euclid’s Elements (Books VII–IX) and the medieval European university curriculum inaugurated by Boethius, Nicomachus composed the foundational treatise that defined number as the active cosmic exemplar in the Mind of the Creator, unifying discrete arithmetic with musical consonance, figurate geometry, and three-dimensional polyhedral symmetry.

Through this digital edition, the mathematical and philosophical architecture of the Introductio is extracted into addressable semantic elements across four synchronized codex tracks. This includes Nicomachus’s ontological charter of the Quadrivium (bifurcating Quantity into discrete Multitude [poson] and continuous Magnitude [pelikon] and establishing Arithmetic’s priority through synanairein co-removal); the deterministic unfolding of all ten species of rational inequality from primordial Equality (\(1 : 1 : 1\)) via the three commands (prostagmata) and sibling reversal (anastrophe); the construction of polygonal numbers via arithmetic gnomons and the discovery of the Sum-of-Cubes theorem (\(\sum k^3 = T_n^2\)); and the expansion of musical means into the ten Neo-Pythagorean proportions culminating in the Cube’s Most Perfect Proportion (\(6 : 8 :: 9 : 12\)), which unites the face-vertex-edge topology of the hexahedron with the fundamental musical intervals (\(2:1, 3:2, 4:3, 9:8\)).

Within the wider GEOMETOR ecosystem, Introductio Arithmetica serves as the primordial arithmetic ground for the constructive canon of Euclid’s Elements (), the projective harmonic configurations of Pappus of Alexandria (), the geometric proportions catalogued in the Encyclopedia of Triangle Centers (geometor.etc), and the operational complexity measures of Émile Lemoine’s Géométrographie (geometor.lemoine). Every ratio unfolding tree, figurate pebble arrangement, and harmonic mean relation is formalized in exact symbolic algebra through geometor.model over \(\mathbb{Q}\).

Sources, Provenance & Methodology¶

This Digital Scriptorium edition of Nicomachus of Gerasa’s Introductio Arithmetica rests on three public-domain pillars documented in resources/introductio_arithmetica/sources_manifest.json:

  1. Greek Critical Edition — Richard Hoche (Teubner, 1866): Richard Hoche, Nicomachi Geraseni Pythagorei Introductionis Arithmeticae Libri II (Leipzig: B. G. Teubner, 1866; hoche:1866). Established from the oldest extant witnesses—notably Codex Cizensis (11th/12th c.), Göttingensis (G), and Monacensis (M)—Hoche’s pagination (pp. 1–72 for Book I, pp. 73–147 for Book II) remains the universal scholarly reference standard. Our Greek -el track stages the SHA-256 verified Open Greek and Latin First1KGreek TEI-XML witness (tlg0358.tlg001.1st1K-grc1.xml) audited directly against 300 DPI scans of Hoche’s 1866 facsimile.

  2. English Translation & Mathematical Studies — D’Ooge, Robbins & Karpinski (Michigan / Macmillan, 1926): Nicomachus of Gerasa: Introduction to Arithmetic, translated into English by Martin Luther D'Ooge, with studies in Greek arithmetic by Frank Egleston Robbins and Louis Charles Karpinski (University of Michigan Studies, Humanistic Series, Vol. XVI, New York: Macmillan, 1926; dooge:1926, public domain in the United States). D’Ooge’s lucid translation (pp. 181–286) and Robbins’s critical footnotes (such as correcting the transmitted exemplars \(4, 7, 8\) and \(3, 5, 6\) in II.28.3–4 to \(4, 6, 7\) and \(4, 6, 9\)) anchor our -en and -notes tracks.

  3. Latin Quadrivium Parallel — Boethius (ed. Friedlein, Teubner, 1867): Around 500 CE, Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius adapted Nicomachus’s Greek treatise into Latin as De Institutione Arithmetica Libri Duo (boethius:friedlein:1867), coining the enduring Latin noun quadruvium (“fourfold way”) at I.1 (p. 7.25 Friedlein) to translate Nicomachus’s four “bridges” (gephyrai) and “ladders” (klimakes) of I.3.6. Every Codex unit cross-references Boethius’s corresponding chapters alongside Iamblichus (iamblichus:pistelli:1894), John Philoponus (philoponus:hoche:1864), Thomas Aquinas (aquinas:in-boethium-trinitate), Luca Pacioli (pacioli:divina-proportione:1509), and Johannes Kepler.
