mission¶

Why Nicomachus of Gerasa Anchors Arithmetic, Harmonics, and the Quadrivium in GEOMETOR

Between the axiomatic deduction of Euclid's Elements (Books VII–IX) and the medieval Latin university curriculum initiated by Boethius, Nicomachus of Gerasa (c. 60–120 CE) composed the single treatise that defined number as the intelligible exemplar in the Mind of the Creator (hoche:1866, I.6.1) and unified discrete arithmetic with musical consonance, polygonal form, and three-dimensional polyhedral symmetry.

Four Pillars of geometor/nicomachus¶

  1. The Ontological Charter of the Quadrivium (Book I, Chs. 1–6): Quoting Archytas of Tarentum (archytas:dk47b1) and Plato's Timaeus and Republic VII (plato:rep:vii-525a-534e), Nicomachus proves that Quantity bifurcates into discrete Multitude (poson) and continuous Magnitude (pelikon). Multitude considered in itself yields Arithmetic, while multitude in relation yields Music; magnitude at rest yields Geometry, while magnitude in revolution yields Astronomy. By Aristotle's criterion of asymmetric co-removal, Arithmetic precedes all three sister sciences as their “mother” (mētēr).

  2. Every Inequality Unfolded from Equality (Book I, Ch. 23 & Book II, Chs. 1–2): Through the three commands (prostagmata) \((a, b, c) \mapsto (a, a+b, a+2b+c)\) combined with sibling anastrophe reversal \((c, b, a)\), Nicomachus demonstrates that every rational ratio—multiple, superparticular, superpartient, and compound—streams forth deterministically from a single root of Equality \((1, 1, 1)\), and reduces uniquely back to \((1, 1, 1)\) under inverse subtraction.

  3. Figurate Alpha-Gnomons, Same/Other & Sum of Cubes (Book II, Chs. 6–20): Representing units by alpha-pebbles (α), Nicomachus builds all \(s\)-gonal numbers by arithmetic gnomons of common difference \(s - 2\), weaves Plato's Same (\(n^2\)) and Other (\(n(n+1)\)) into universal arithmetic harmonies, and discovers that successive blocks of odd numbers sum to cubes \(k^3\) whose cumulative area equals the squared triangular number \(T_n^2\).

  4. The Ten Means & the Cube’s Most Perfect Proportion (Book II, Chs. 21–29): Expanding the three classical means (Arithmetic, Geometric, Harmonic) into the ten Neo-Pythagorean proportions, Nicomachus culminates in \(6 : 8 :: 9 : 12\)—where the Cube’s \(6\) faces, \(8\) vertices, and \(12\) edges encode the harmonic (\(8\)) and arithmetic (\(9\)) means and every Pythagorean consonance (\(2:1, 3:2, 4:3, 9:8\)).
