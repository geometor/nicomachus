=====================================================================
Chapter 6: Alpha-Pebble Notation, the Monad, and the Three Dimensions
=====================================================================

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0252
   :page: p. 236

**1.** Thus far we have spoken sufficiently concerning relative quantity, selecting what is fitting and readily grasped by beginners. What remains on that subject will be filled in once we have paused to set forth earlier matters that belong to absolute quantity and have prior claim—for mathematical theorems ever illuminate one another.
What we must first examine concerns linear, plane, and solid numbers—cubic and spherical, equilateral and scalene, bricks, beams, wedges, and the like—which properly belong to geometry as concerning magnitude, yet are taken up seminally into arithmetic as their mother and elder; for we remember how arithmetic

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0253
   :page: p. 237

recently proved to abolish the other sciences with itself while not being abolished with them, and to be implied necessarily by them while not implying them.

**2.** First, we must recognize that every letter by which we mark a number—such as :math:`\iota` for ten, :math:`\kappa` for twenty, :math:`\omega` for eight hundred—signifies the number by human convention and agreement, not by nature.
The natural, artless, and therefore simplest notation for numbers is the side-by-side display of the units contained in each: thus one unit written by a single *alpha* will signify :math:`1`; two units side by side, two *alphas*, will signify :math:`2`; three set in a straight line will be the mark of :math:`3`; four in line, of :math:`4`; five, of :math:`5`; and so onward. Only through such writing can the shapes of planes and solids be clearly shown:

   The monad ``α``, the dyad ``αα``, the triad ``ααα``, the tetrad ``αααα``, the pentad ``ααααα``,

and similarly for greater numbers.
**3.** Now the :term:`monad <monas>`, holding the place and character of a point, will be the principle of intervals and numbers, though not yet an interval or a number—just as a point is the principle of line and interval, though not yet line or interval. Indeed, point added to point makes nothing greater, for the dimensionless joined to the dimensionless gains no dimension, as though one added nothing to nothing,

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0254
   :page: p. 238

which produces nothing. We saw the same with :term:`equality <isotes>` among ratios: proportion is preserved—as first to second, so second to third—yet no interval is generated between the extremes, as happens in all unequal ratios. Likewise the monad alone among all numbers, when multiplied by itself, generates nothing greater than itself.
**4.** Thus the monad is dimensionless and foundational; interval is first discovered in the dyad, then in the triad, the tetrad, and those that follow, since an interval is what is beheld between two terms. The first interval is called *line*—what is extended in one dimension; two intervals make *surface*—extended in two dimensions; three intervals make *solid*—extended in three dimensions. Nor can we conceive a solid with more than three dimensions: depth, breadth, and length. By these are defined the six directions belonging to every body, along which spatial motions are distinguished—forward and back, up and down, right and left—two opposite directions accompanying each dimension.

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0255
   :page: p. 239

**5.** The statement also converts: whatever is solid has all three dimensions—length, depth, and breadth—and whatever has three dimensions is necessarily solid and nothing else. What has only two dimensions is not solid, but *surface*, admitting two dimensions alone; here too the converse holds: surface is what is two-dimensional, and what is two-dimensional is necessarily surface. Thus surface falls short of solid by one dimension; line, being one-dimensional, falls short of surface by one dimension and of solid by two; and the point falls short of line by one dimension—being dimensionless, falling short of solid by three dimensions, of surface by two, and of line by one.
