=========================================================
Chapter 15: Cubes, Beams, Bricks, Wedges & Scalene Solids
=========================================================

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0268
   :page: p. 252

**1.** Such is the generation, progression, growth, and nature of the equilateral pyramidal solids, having for their seed and root the polygonal numbers themselves and their orderly stacking. Yet there exists another orderly family of solids of a different kind—those named *cubes*, *beams*, *bricks*, *wedges*, *spherical* numbers, and *parallelepipeds*—whose order of progression runs as follows.

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0269
   :page: p. 253

**2.** The squares mentioned earlier—1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, and the rest—being two-dimensional and possessing only length and breadth in plane representation, take on a third dimension as well and become solid, three-dimensional numbers when each is multiplied by its own side: 4, being twice 2, is taken twice again to complete 8; 9, being thrice 3, is increased by 3 in a further dimension to become 27; 16, being four times 4, is enlarged again by its own side 4 to become 64; and the rest follow in the same fashion without end. Here too the edges will contain just as many units as the sides of the squares from which they sprang, each matching its own: the edges of 8 consist of two units, matching 4; those of 27 of three units, matching 9; those of 64 of four units, matching 16; and so on in sequence, so that the edge of unity—the cube in potentiality—will likewise be unity on every side, just like that of unity as the square in potentiality.

.. folio:: introductio_arithmetica:0270
   :page: p. 254

**3.** Universally, every square is a single plane surface and possesses four angles and four sides; whereas every cube, having grown from each square multiplied by its own side, will have six plane faces in all, each equal to its parent square, twelve edges, each equal and containing the same count of units as the side of its parent square, and eight solid angles, each enclosed by three edges of the same measure as each edge of its parent square.
